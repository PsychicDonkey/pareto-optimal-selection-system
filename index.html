Selection decision support

Pareto Selection Planner

Choose which assessments go into a selection battery and how to weight them, and see what each choice costs in hire quality and adverse impact.

The problem, and the answer

S

Situation

Hundreds of hires a year, decided by one combined score.

Large South African employers hire in bulk: graduate programmes, learnerships, branch and contact-centre roles. Each applicant takes several assessments, and the scores are combined into one.

C

Complication

The best predictors carry the biggest group gaps.

The tests that best predict performance also show the largest score differences between groups. Lean on them and you hire stronger performers, but fewer people from the focal group. Most teams only see this after the intake.

Q

Question

Can you see your options before anyone is hired?

The person designing the assessments needs to see every reasonable choice, what each one costs, and pick one they can defend.

A · Answer

The Pareto Selection Planner

See every best trade-off up front; the choice stays with you.

1 · Solves the problem for the user

Before the intake, the assessment designer sees how much hiring quality each option gives up for fairer outcomes. The choice becomes explicit, with a record to justify it under the Employment Equity Act.

2 · How it works

Pick the assessments. The optimisation engine, which is the AI in the tool, finds the best weighting for every possible trade-off and draws them as a frontier. Choose a point, then score real applicants.

3 · How we know it works

It reproduces the published results of De Corte et al. (2007) and Berry et al. (2024) to within .01. The Checks tab shows the comparison.

Who it is for

The I-O psychologist or talent assessment lead at a large South African employer who designs the assessment battery for a high-volume intake, such as a graduate programme, learnership, or branch or contact-centre hiring, and who must be able to justify that battery under the Employment Equity Act.

When they use it. Once per intake design cycle, before applications open.

What they do today. Choose tests by vendor default or validity alone, weight them equally or by judgement, and check adverse impact after the intake. The trade-off stays invisible until it has happened, and there is no record of the options considered.

Caveat: simulation only

The values are mostly US meta-analytic estimates and are suitable for simulation only. Section 8 of the Employment Equity Act requires that assessments be shown to be valid and reliable, applied fairly and not biased against any employee or group. Real use requires South African validation studies, local norms or South African meta-analytic evidence (see Theron, 2009).

Live demo: three moves

  1. Change the battery. Add or drop an assessment, for example drop cognitive ability, and watch the frontier move.
  2. Choose a trade-off. Set the adverse impact threshold, or click a point on the frontier, and read what that option costs.
  3. Score an applicant file. Load the example pool and show who would be hired, by group, under the chosen weights.

What changed from previous versions

  • A predictor library of up to seven assessments, any two or more of which can form the battery.
  • An exact optimiser replaces the grid search, so the frontier is precise for any battery size.
  • Focal and reference groups replace minority and majority, with a 60% focal default.
  • An adjustable adverse impact threshold instead of a fixed four-fifths line.
  • Click anywhere in the trade-off chart; dominated weightings show what they leave on the table.
  • Raw-score uploads with an editable norm table.
  • A second known-answer test against Berry et al. (2024) Table 5.

Extension: other group comparisons

Gender (men–women) comparisons are a natural extension, but published men–women d values across these predictors are patchy, so the tool models Black–White differences only. The engine supports any two-group comparison once defensible d values exist.

        Checks

        Everything that exists to verify the results: two known-answer tests against published tables, cross-checks of the optimiser, matrix validity, simulation against the analytic model, and edge cases. Checks 3 to 7 use the battery and weights currently set on the Dashboard.

        Method and assumptions
        • Predictors and criterion are multivariate normal with the same correlation matrix in both groups; groups differ only in means. All values are in within-group SD units with the focal mean at 0.
        • Selection is top-down on the weighted composite with one common cut-off; group membership is never used to rank.
        • Weights are non-negative and sum to 1.
        • Expected criterion score is the mean criterion score of those selected, standardised to the whole applicant pool (De Corte et al., 2007, Appendix A). The AI ratio is the focal selection rate divided by the reference selection rate.
        • Frontier computed exactly by weighted-sum scalarisation: for each relative weight on validity and composite d, the best non-negative weighting is the solution of a small non-negative quadratic programme, solved by checking the optimality conditions on every set of active predictors. With a positive-definite matrix every point of the frontier is reached this way. Checked against a brute-force grid, random weightings, De Corte et al. (2007) Table 2 and Berry et al. (2024) Table 5.
        • Values mix corrected validities with d values from mixed applicant and incumbent samples; the SJT toggle illustrates how much that matters.
